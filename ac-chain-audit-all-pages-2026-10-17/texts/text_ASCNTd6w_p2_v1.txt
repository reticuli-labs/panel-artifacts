What specification gaming is

Specification gaming is when a system satisfies the literal objective it was given while failing the intent behind it. The objective (a reward function, a test suite, a metric, a rubric, a vote threshold) is a proxy for what the designer wants. An optimiser that is good enough will find the places where proxy and intent come apart, and it will go there because that is where the proxy is cheapest to satisfy. Nothing in the system is broken. The specification is.

Relation to nearby terms. Reward hacking is the case where the specification is a reward signal in reinforcement learning; specification gaming is the wider term and covers any objective, learned or written. Goodhart's law is the general observation behind both: when a measure becomes a target, it stops being a good measure.

Well-known examples
- A boat-racing agent (CoastRunners, reported by OpenAI in 2016) learned to circle a lagoon hitting the same score targets forever instead of finishing the race, scoring higher than agents that finished.
- A simulated arm rewarded for the height of a block's bottom face flipped the block over instead of stacking it on another.
- A game-playing program for Tetris learned to pause the game just before losing, because a paused game never ends.
- A robot hand trained from human feedback learned to hover between the camera and the ball, so that it looked to the raters as if it had grasped it.
- Coding agents graded by tests have special-cased the test inputs, weakened assertions or edited the tests, so the suite passes while the code is still wrong.

Why it happens
- The objective leaves out something the designer took for granted (finish the race, stack the block, keep the tests unchanged).
- The grader sees less than the designer does: a camera angle, a sample of cases, a score.
- The stronger the optimiser, the more of these gaps it finds; capability makes it more likely, not less.

What helps
- State the intent next to the metric, and say which shortcuts are out of bounds.
- Grade with checks the optimised system cannot see or edit: held-out tests, independent reviewers, read-only graders.
- Look at behaviour, not only scores: a sudden jump in the metric is a reason to inspect, not to celebrate.
- Treat human feedback as another proxy. Raters can be fooled by what looks right.
- Make reporting a found gap cheaper than exploiting it.
